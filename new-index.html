Getting Started

Malayalam for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Malayalam is the language of Kerala, the green coastal state in the south-west of India, and it is spoken by well over 38 million people. It belongs to the Dravidian language family, which makes it a close relative of Tamil, Kannada and Telugu rather than of Hindi. If you have ever heard Malayalam described as a tongue-twister, do not let that put you off: its grammar is logical, its spelling is remarkably consistent, and a small set of everyday words will carry you through most daily situations.

A language that flows

Malayalam is famous for the speed and rhythm of its speech and for long, flowing words built by joining smaller pieces together. It is also the source of a favourite trivia fact: "Malayalam" reads the same forwards and backwards — a perfect palindrome. The language has been written for many centuries and has a rich tradition of poetry, cinema and journalism; Kerala's literacy rate is the highest in India, and newspapers in Malayalam are read everywhere from Kochi to the Gulf.

How Malayalam sentences are built

The biggest difference from English is word order. English follows Subject–Verb–Object ("I eat rice"), while Malayalam follows Subject–Object–Verb, so the same idea comes out as "I rice eat" — ഞാൻ ചോറ് കഴിക്കുന്നു (njaan choru kazhikkunnu). Once you expect the verb at the end, Malayalam sentences quickly stop feeling backwards.

A few more patterns worth knowing:

  • No capital letters. The Malayalam script does not use capitals at all.
  • Questions are often marked by a suffix. Adding -ഓ (-o) to a word can turn a statement into a yes/no question.
  • Respect is built in. There are different words for "you" depending on how formal you want to be; നിങ്ങൾ (ningal) is the polite choice with strangers and elders.
  • Verbs agree loosely. Unlike many languages, Malayalam verbs do not change for person or gender, which is one less thing to memorise.

The first words to learn

Start with the words you will use every single day. Greetings come first: നമസ്കാരം (namaskaram, hello), സുപ്രഭാതം (suprabhatham, good morning) and നന്ദി (nandi, thank you). Add the question words — എന്ത് (enthu, what), എവിടെ (evide, where), എപ്പോൾ (eppol, when), എങ്ങനെ (engane, how) and എന്തുകൊണ്ട് (enthukondu, why) — and you can already ask about almost anything. Numbers, days of the week and words for food, water, home and family come next; you will find many of them on our Common Phrases page.

Learning the script

Many beginners delay learning the Malayalam script and rely on English letters instead. That works for a holiday, but reading the real script unlocks signboards, menus and messages, and it makes pronunciation far more accurate because Malayalam spelling maps closely to sound. The script looks intricate, yet it is built from a tidy system of vowels and consonants — our guide to the Malayalam alphabet walks through it step by step.

A realistic study routine

  1. Spend ten minutes a day translating sentences you actually say — use the translator and read the Malayalam result aloud.
  2. Learn five new words a day from the phrasebook rather than long lists you will forget.
  3. Watch short Malayalam film scenes or songs with subtitles; your ear learns the rhythm before your brain learns the rules.
  4. Practise with a Malayali friend or colleague — even a cheerful സുഖമാണോ? (sukhamano? — how are you?) is appreciated.

Above all, do not aim for perfection at the start. Malayalam speakers are famously encouraging to learners, and a few honest sentences in their language will earn you smiles — and often a plate of something delicious — anywhere in Kerala.

The Script

The Malayalam Alphabet: Understanding the Script

The Malayalam script is one of the most graceful writing systems in the world — a cascade of loops and curves in which almost every sound of the language has its own sign. It is an abugida: consonants carry an in-built "a" sound, and small marks added to them change that vowel. Once you understand that single idea, the whole script falls into place.

The vowels (സ്വരങ്ങൾ)

Notice the pairs: most vowels come in a short and a long version, and the length changes meaning, so it is worth training your ear early. The last two signs, അം (am) and അഃ (ah), add a nasal or breathy ending to a syllable.

The consonants (വ്യഞ്ജനങ്ങൾ)

There are 36 consonant letters, traditionally arranged in neat rows by where they are pronounced in the mouth — from the throat (ക വർഗ്ഗം) to the lips (പ വർഗ്ഗം) — exactly the way Sanskrit grammarians organised them over a thousand years ago.

Every consonant on its own is read with a short "a": ക is "ka", മ is "ma", പ is "pa". To change the vowel you add a sign: കി ki, കു ku, കൂ koo, കെ ke, കോ ko. To remove the vowel entirely — for a consonant at the end of a word — a small mark called the chandrakkala is used: ക് is a bare "k".

Chillu letters

Malayalam has a special set of "chillu" letters: pure consonants that appear at the ends of words without a vowel and without the chandrakkala mark. The common ones are ൻ (n), ർ (r), ൽ (l), ൾ (zh-like l) and ൺ (ṇ). You will meet them constantly — the word അവൻ (avan, "he") ends with a chillu. Older printing sometimes wrote these with the chandrakkala instead, so both spellings exist in books.

Joined letters (conjuncts)

When two consonants meet with no vowel between them, Malayalam often fuses them into a single stacked or joined shape: ക്ക (kka), ട്ട (tta), ന്ത (ntha), മ്പ (mpa). There are many of these, and they are the main reason Malayalam text can look dense to a beginner. The good news: you do not need to memorise them one by one. Reading them as "first consonant + chandrakkala + second consonant" works, and your eye learns the frequent shapes quickly with practice.

A famous reform

In 1971 the Government of Kerala simplified the official script for school teaching and printing, reducing the number of conjunct shapes children had to learn. Books and newspapers printed before and after the reform can look slightly different, and Malayalam has one of the largest letter inventories of any Indian script — a point of pride, and the reason a good Malayalam font like Noto Sans Malayalam (used on this site) matters for correct display.

Try it: pick any sentence from our Common Phrases page and hunt for the letters you have just learned. Spotting ക, മ and ൻ in real words is the fastest way to make the script feel familiar.

Better Results

How to Translate English to Malayalam Accurately: 9 Practical Tips

Machine translation between English and Malayalam has improved enormously, but the two languages are built very differently, so a little care on your side produces noticeably better results. These nine tips come from the mistakes we see most often.

1. Write complete sentences

A translator guesses far better from "Please send me the report tomorrow morning" than from a bare fragment like "report tomorrow". Full sentences give the engine the grammar it needs to choose the right Malayalam word order — verb at the end, postpositions after nouns.

2. Keep sentences short

One idea per sentence. A 60-word English sentence packed with clauses often becomes tangled in Malayalam. Split it into two or three sentences and the translation becomes cleaner and easier to check.

3. Use punctuation properly

Commas and full stops are signposts. They tell the translator where one thought ends and the next begins, and they strongly affect where Malayalam places its own connectors.

4. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word-for-word sounds bizarre in any language. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "It is raining very heavily" — then translate. The same goes for office jargon and abbreviations.

5. Be careful with names and places

Names should normally stay as they are, written in Malayalam script by sound. Check that a person's name has been transliterated rather than translated — and that a place name such as Kochi or Wayanad has not been turned into a common word.

6. Choose the right level of respect

English "you" hides a decision Malayalam forces you to make: നീ (nee) for close friends and children, നിങ്ങൾ (ningal) as the safe polite form, and honorific constructions for elders and officials. If your message is formal, translate a clearly polite English sentence and prefer the നിങ്ങൾ forms in the result.

7. Watch out for English words with several meanings

"Book" can be a thing you read or the act of reserving; "light" can be weight, brightness or a lamp. If the wrong meaning appears, add a clarifying word to your English sentence ("book a ticket", "light the lamp") and translate again.

8. Translate paragraph by paragraph

For letters and documents, work one paragraph at a time and read each Malayalam result before moving on. This keeps errors local and lets you fix names and numbers while the context is fresh. Keep each chunk under the tool's 2,000-character limit.

9. Back-translate to check

Paste the Malayalam result into the translator, swap the direction, and translate it back into English. If the back-translation keeps your meaning, you are in good shape. If it drifts, adjust the original English wording and try again. For legal, medical or official documents, always have a native speaker review the final text — no machine tool should be the last word there.

Travel

Essential Malayalam Phrases for Travelling in Kerala

Kerala — "God's Own Country" — rewards travellers who make even a small effort with the local language. English is widely understood in hotels and cities, but a greeting in Malayalam at a tea stall, a bus stop or a houseboat jetty changes the whole conversation. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Start every interaction with നമസ്കാരം (namaskaram — hello), hands together if you like. Ask സുഖമാണോ? (sukhamano? — how are you?) and answer സുഖമാണ് (sukham aanu — I am fine). Thank people with നന്ദി (nandi), apologise with ക്ഷമിക്കണം (kshamikkanam — excuse me / sorry), and say goodbye with പോയി വരാം (poyi varaam — I'll go and come back), the warm Malayalam way of taking leave.

Getting around

For directions you need എവിടെയാണ്? (evideyaanu? — where is it?) combined with your destination: റെയിൽവേ സ്റ്റേഷൻ എവിടെയാണ്? (railway station evideyaanu?). In an auto-rickshaw, നേരെ പോകൂ (nere poku — go straight), ഇടത്തേക്ക് തിരിയൂ (idathekku thiriyu — turn left) and ഇവിടെ നിർത്തൂ (ivide nirthu — stop here) will serve you well. Always agree the fare first with എത്രയാണ്? (ethrayaanu? — how much is it?).

Food and drink

Kerala's food is a highlight of any trip. Ask for water with വെള്ളം തരൂ (vellam tharu — please give me water), order tea with ഒരു ചായ (oru chaya — one tea), and say എരിവ് കുറച്ച് (erivu kurachu — less spicy) if your tolerance is modest. When the meal is wonderful — and it often is — tell your host ഭക്ഷണം വളരെ നല്ലതായിരുന്നു (bhakshanam valare nallathaayirunnu — the food was very good).

Shopping and bargaining

In markets, ഇതിന്റെ വില എത്രയാണ്? (ithinte vila ethrayaanu? — what is the price of this?) opens the conversation. If the price feels high, smile and try വില അല്പം കൂടുതലാണ് (vila alpam kooduthalaanu — the price is a little high) or ask കുറയ്ക്കാമോ? (kuraykkaamo? — can you reduce it?). Finish with ഞാൻ ഇത് എടുക്കാം (njaan ithu edukkaam — I'll take this) when you are happy.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: സഹായിക്കൂ! (sahaayikku! — help!), എനിക്ക് ഒരു ഡോക്ടറെ വേണം (enikku oru doctore venam — I need a doctor) and ആശുപത്രി എവിടെയാണ്? (aashupathri evideyaanu? — where is the hospital?). Kerala's people are quick to help a traveller in trouble, and these phrases make sure the right help arrives fast.

Numbers & Counting

Malayalam Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In Kerala you will use them for bus fares, market prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and train times — often several times a day. The good news is that Malayalam counting follows a clear pattern. Learn the first ten numbers well, and the rest of the system opens up quickly.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: ഒന്ന് (onnu — one), രണ്ട് (randu — two), മൂന്ന് (moonnu — three), നാല് (naalu — four), അഞ്ച് (anchu — five), ആറ് (aaru — six), ഏഴ് (ezhu — seven), എട്ട് (ettu — eight), ഒൻപത് (onpathu — nine) and പത്ത് (pathu — ten). Say them aloud a few times; Malayalam speakers will recognise your effort instantly, and shopkeepers often respond with a smile and a fairer price.

The teens: 11 to 19

The teen numbers are built on ten, but several change their shape a little, so learn them as their own words: പതിനൊന്ന് (pathinonnu — eleven), പന്ത്രണ്ട് (panthrandu — twelve), പതിമൂന്ന് (pathimoonnu — thirteen), പതിനാല് (pathinaalu — fourteen), പതിനഞ്ച് (pathinanchu — fifteen), പതിനാറ് (pathinaaru — sixteen), പതിനേഴ് (pathinezhu — seventeen), പതിനെട്ട് (pathinettu — eighteen) and പത്തൊൻപത് (pathonpathu — nineteen).

The tens: 20 to 100

From twenty onwards the pattern becomes regular again: ഇരുപത് (irupathu — twenty), മുപ്പത് (muppathu — thirty), നാല്പത് (naalpathu — forty), അമ്പത് (ambathu — fifty), അറുപത് (arupathu — sixty), എഴുപത് (ezhupathu — seventy), എൺപത് (enpathu — eighty), തൊണ്ണൂറ് (thonnooru — ninety) and നൂറ് (nooru — one hundred). Numbers in between join the ten and the unit, so twenty-five is ഇരുപത്തിയഞ്ച് (irupathiyanchu) — literally "twenty-and-five".

Hundreds, thousands and lakhs

For bigger numbers: ആയിരം (aayiram — one thousand), പതിനായിരം (pathinaayiram — ten thousand) and ലക്ഷം (laksham — one lakh, that is, one hundred thousand). India counts in lakhs and crores rather than millions, so a house price quoted as അമ്പത് ലക്ഷം (ambathu laksham) means fifty lakh rupees — five million. Knowing this one word prevents expensive misunderstandings.

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: എത്രയാണ്? (ethrayaanu? — how much is it?). Giving your phone number digit by digit is normal in Kerala, and zero is പൂജ്യം (poojyam). For quantities, put the number before the thing: രണ്ട് ചായ (randu chaya — two teas) will be understood in any tea shop in the state.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, cups of tea — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

  • വഴി (vazhi) — road, way

Describing words

  • വലിയ (valiya) — big

  • നല്ല (nalla) — good
  • പുതിയ (puthiya) — new

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Malayalam

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a train, fixing a meeting or planning around the monsoon, you need days, dates and clock times. Malayalam keeps this simple: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make in Kerala.

The seven days

The week runs: ഞായർ (njaayar — Sunday), തിങ്കൾ (thinkal — Monday), ചൊവ്വ (chovva — Tuesday), ബുധൻ (budhan — Wednesday), വ്യാഴം (vyaazham — Thursday), വെള്ളി (velli — Friday) and ശനി (shani — Saturday). Most are named after the sun, moon and planets, just like the English words Sunday and Monday. To say "on Monday", simply add the day to your sentence — Malayalam word order does the rest.

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: ഇന്ന് (innu — today), നാളെ (naale — tomorrow) and ഇന്നലെ (innale — yesterday). Add മറ്റന്നാൾ (matannaal — the day after tomorrow) and you can talk about the whole week. "See you tomorrow" is നാളെ കാണാം (naale kaanaam) — a phrase worth using daily.

Parts of the day

Kerala days are shaped by heat and rain, and the words follow: രാവിലെ (raavile — morning), ഉച്ചയ്ക്ക് (uchaykku — at noon, midday), വൈകുന്നേരം (vaikunneram — evening) and രാത്രി (raathri — night). Greetings follow the same pattern — സുപ്രഭാതം (suprabhaatham) for good morning and ശുഭരാത്രി (shubharaathri) for good night.

Months of the year

For everyday dates, Kerala uses the familiar English month names in Malayalam dress: ജനുവരി (januvari — January) through ഡിസംബർ (disambar — December). But Kerala also keeps its own solar calendar, and you will see its months on festival posters and almanacs: ചിങ്ങം (chingam, mid-August to mid-September) is the first month — Onam falls in Chingam — followed by കന്നി (kanni), തുലാം (thulaam), വൃശ്ചികം (vrishchikam), ധനു (dhanu), മകരം (makaram), കുംഭം (kumbham), മീനം (meenam), മേടം (medam — Vishu falls here), ഇടവം (idavam), മിഥുനം (mithunam) and കർക്കിടകം (karkidakam), the rainy month.

Asking and telling the time

Ask എത്ര മണിയായി? (ethra maniyaayi? — what time is it?, literally "how many hours have become?"). Answers use മണി (mani — o'clock): പത്ത് മണി (pathu mani — ten o'clock). Half past is അര (ara): പത്തര (pathara) is half past ten. An hour is ഒരു മണിക്കൂർ (oru manikkoor) and a minute is ഒരു മിനിറ്റ് (oru minittu), borrowed neatly from English.

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the state. For longer sentences — "The train leaves on Friday at half past six" — draft it in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family

Family Words in Malayalam: Relatives and Relationships

Family sits at the centre of life in Kerala, and Malayalam has a precise word for almost every relationship — including some that English squeezes into a single word like "uncle" or "cousin". Learning these words does more than build vocabulary: it helps you understand how people address each other, and why a neighbour's child may call you "uncle" or "aunty" with complete sincerity.

Parents and grandparents

Father is അച്ഛൻ (achan) and mother is അമ്മ (amma) — two of the most loved words in the language. Grandfather is മുത്തച്ഛൻ (muthachan) and grandmother is മുത്തശ്ശി (muthashi). In many homes grandparents are also called അപ്പൂപ്പൻ (appooppan) and അമ്മൂമ്മ (ammoomma), words children find easier to say. The general word for family itself is കുടുംബം (kudumbam).

Brothers and sisters

Malayalam always asks: older or younger? An elder brother is ചേട്ടൻ (chettan) and an elder sister is ചേച്ചി (chechi) — words you will also hear used for any slightly older man or woman, related or not. A younger brother is അനിയൻ (aniyan) and a younger sister is അനിയത്തി (aniyathi). The neutral words are സഹോദരൻ (sahodaran — brother) and സഹോദരി (sahodari — sister), used in writing and formal speech.

Uncles and aunts

Here Malayalam is more detailed than English. Your mother's brother is അമ്മാവൻ (ammaavan), while your father's elder brother is വലിയച്ഛൻ (valiyachan — literally "big father") and his younger brother is കൊച്ചച്ഛൻ (kochachan — "little father"). Aunts follow the same pattern with അമ്മായി (ammaayi) for several of these relationships. Do not worry about memorising every branch at once; Keralites are forgiving, and അങ്കിൾ (uncle) and ആന്റി (aunty) are used happily in daily life.

Children and the next generation

A son is മകൻ (makan) and a daughter is മകൾ (makal); a child of any age is കുട്ടി (kutti), and children are കുട്ടികൾ (kuttikal). Grandchildren are പേരക്കുട്ടികൾ (perakkuttikal). A husband is ഭർത്താവ് (bharthaavu) and a wife is ഭാര്യ (bhaarya); parents-in-law are respectfully അമ്മായിയച്ഛൻ (ammaayiyachan — father-in-law) and അമ്മായിയമ്മ (ammaayiyamma — mother-in-law).

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is ഇത് എന്റെ അമ്മയാണ് (ithu ente ammayaanu). "I have two brothers" is എനിക്ക് രണ്ട് സഹോദരന്മാരുണ്ട് (enikku randu sahodaranmaaru undu). Notice എന്റെ (ente — my) and എനിക്ക് (enikku — to me, I have) doing the heavy lifting; both return in our self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in Kerala, not prying — asking about someone's കുടുംബം shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in India.

Speaking

Malayalam Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in Kerala begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

The all-purpose namaskaram

നമസ്കാരം (namaskaram) works at any hour, for any age, in any setting — hello, good day and respectful greeting in one word. Join your palms lightly as you say it and you have the complete Kerala greeting. When someone greets you first, simply return the same word with a smile. For extra respect when addressing elders, the word alone, said slowly and clearly, is perfect.

Greetings by time of day

Malayalam also has time-specific greetings, used in slightly more formal moments: സുപ്രഭാതം (suprabhaatham — good morning), ശുഭ സായാഹ്നം (shubha saayaanham — good evening) and ശുഭരാത്രി (shubharaathri — good night, said when parting at night or before sleep). In casual daily life, namaskaram plus a smile is far more common than all three.

Asking how someone is

The standard question is സുഖമാണോ? (sukhamano? — are you well?). The usual answer is സുഖമാണ് (sukhamaanu — I am fine), often followed by returning the question: നിങ്ങൾക്ക് സുഖമാണോ? (ningalkku sukhamano? — and you, are you well?). Between close friends you will hear the shorter എന്തുണ്ട് വിശേഷം? (enthundu vishesham? — what's new?).

On the phone

Phone calls open with ഹലോ (halo — hello), borrowed from English and used by everyone. To ask who is speaking: ആരാണ് സംസാരിക്കുന്നത്? (aaraanu samsaarikkunnathu?). To say "I will call back", use ഞാൻ തിരികെ വിളിക്കാം (njaan thirike vilikkaam). Wrong numbers end politely with ക്ഷമിക്കണം (kshamikkanam — sorry).

Welcome and congratulations

Welcome a guest with സ്വാഗതം (swaagatham — welcome). Congratulate with അഭിനന്ദനങ്ങൾ (abhinandanangal — congratulations), wish a birthday with ജന്മദിനാശംസകൾ (janmadinaashamsakal — birthday greetings), and thank someone warmly with വളരെ നന്ദി (valare nandi — thank you very much). Festival wishes get their own guide — see our festivals article.

Saying goodbye — Kerala style

There is no single everyday word for "goodbye"; instead people say പോയി വരട്ടെ (poyi varatte — "shall I go and come back?"), which the host answers with പോയി വരൂ (poyi varoo — go and come). Between friends, പിന്നെ കാണാം (pinne kaanaam — see you later) is the natural parting. The idea underneath is lovely: nobody really leaves; they only go and return.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Malayalam: Market Phrases That Work

Kerala's markets — the morning fish market, the banana-chip shops, the textile stores before Onam — run on friendly talk. Prices in small shops are often a conversation, not a label. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with നമസ്കാരം (namaskaram). If you are just browsing, say ഞാൻ വെറുതെ നോക്കുകയാണ് (njaan veruthe nokkukayaanu — I am just looking). To ask for something specific: കാണിക്കൂ (kaanikku — please show me) followed by the item, or ഇതിന്റെ മറ്റൊരു നിറം ഉണ്ടോ? (ithinte mattoru niram undo? — do you have this in another colour?). The word for shop is കട (kada) and for market, ചന്ത (chantha).

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: ഇതിന്റെ വില എത്രയാണ്? (ithinte vila ethrayaanu? — what is the price of this?). For things sold by weight, ask കിലോ എത്രയാണ്? (kilo ethrayaanu? — how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, ഒന്നു കൂടി പറയൂ (onnu koodi parayoo — please say it once more) buys you a slower repeat.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: വലിയ സൈസ് ഉണ്ടോ? (valiya size undo? — do you have a bigger size?) and ചെറിയ സൈസ് (cheriya size — smaller size). Common colours: ചുവപ്പ് (chuvappu — red), നീല (neela — blue), പച്ച (pacha — green), കറുപ്പ് (karuppu — black), വെളുപ്പ് (veluppu — white) and മഞ്ഞ (manja — yellow). To try something on, ask ഇട്ടു നോക്കാമോ? (ittu nokkaamo? — may I try it on?).

Bargaining, politely

In markets (less so in fixed-price shops), gentle bargaining is expected. Open with വില അല്പം കൂടുതലാണ് (vila alpam kooduthalaanu — the price is a little high), then ask കുറയ്ക്കാമോ? (kuraykkaamo? — can you reduce it?). If the seller will not move, അവസാന വില എത്രയാണ്? (avasaana vila ethrayaanu? — what is your final price?) settles it. Accept with ശരി, ഞാൻ ഇത് എടുക്കാം (shari, njaan ithu edukkaam — okay, I will take this), or decline kindly with വേണ്ട, നന്ദി (venda, nandi — no need, thank you).

Paying and finishing

Ask for the bill with ബിൽ തരൂ (bill tharoo). Cash is കാശ് (kaashu); if you hand over a large note, the change you receive back is ബാക്കി (baakki). Most shops now accept UPI payments — യുപിഐ ഉണ്ടോ? (UPI undo? — do you take UPI?) is a perfectly normal question in 2026.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Malayalam, even imperfect Malayalam, changes the whole shopping experience.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Malayalam

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. Kerala has good hospitals and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The word that cuts through noise is സഹായിക്കൂ! (sahaayikku! — help!). Add അപകടം! (apakadam! — danger! / accident!) if there has been a crash or a fall. To stop someone or something urgently: നിർത്തൂ! (nirthoo! — stop!). India's single emergency number is 112, and an ambulance can also be reached on 108 in Kerala — numbers worth saving in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: ആശുപത്രി എവിടെയാണ്? (aashupathri evideyaanu? — where is the hospital?), ഒരു ഡോക്ടറെ വിളിക്കൂ (oru doctore vilikku — please call a doctor), and ആംബുലൻസ് വിളിക്കൂ (ambulance vilikku — call an ambulance). If you cannot travel, say എനിക്ക് നടക്കാൻ കഴിയില്ല (enikku nadakkaan kazhiyilla — I cannot walk). The word for a doctor is ഡോക്ടർ (doctor) and for a hospital, ആശുപത്രി (aashupathri).

Describing what is wrong

Start with എനിക്ക് സുഖമില്ല (enikku sukhamilla — I am not well). Point and name the problem: വേദന (vedana — pain) combines with body parts — തലവേദന (thalavedana — headache), വയറുവേദന (vayaruvedana — stomach ache), പല്ലുവേദന (palluvedana — toothache). Other common words: പനി (pani — fever), ചുമ (chuma — cough), ഛർദ്ദി (charddi — vomiting), രക്തം (raktham — blood) and അലർജി (alarji — allergy). "It hurts here" is ഇവിടെ വേദനയുണ്ട് (ivide vedanayundu) — point as you say it.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is മരുന്ന് (marunnu), and a pharmacy is usually signed "Medicals" or മരുന്നുകട (marunnukada). Useful lines: ഈ മരുന്ന് ഉണ്ടോ? (ee marunnu undo? — do you have this medicine?), ദിവസത്തിൽ എത്ര തവണ കഴിക്കണം? (divasathil ethra thavana kazhikkanam? — how many times a day should I take it?) and ഭക്ഷണത്തിന് മുമ്പോ ശേഷമോ? (bhakshanathinu mumpbo sheshamo? — before food or after?).

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: പോലീസിനെ വിളിക്കൂ (policine vilikku — call the police). "I have lost my bag" is എന്റെ ബാഗ് നഷ്ടപ്പെട്ടു (ente bag nashtappettu), and "I don't understand" — എനിക്ക് മനസ്സിലായില്ല (enikku manassilaayilla) — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed.

Grammar Basics

Malayalam Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Vocabulary lists teach you words; question words teach you how to learn more words. With six question words and a handful of pronouns, you can ask your way through Kerala — and understand the answers people give you. This guide gives you both sets, then shows how they combine into real questions.

The question words

  • എന്ത് (enthu) — what
  • ആര് (aaru) — who
  • എവിടെ (evide) — where
  • എപ്പോൾ (eppol) — when
  • എന്തുകൊണ്ട് (enthukondu) — why
  • എങ്ങനെ (engane) — how
  • ഏത് (ethu) — which
  • എത്ര (ethra) — how much, how many

Most Malayalam questions end with the verb carrying a questioning ending, often -ഓ (-o): സുഖമാണോ? (sukhamano? — are you well?). You have already met എത്ര in prices and time; it is the hardest-working word in this list.

Personal pronouns

  • ഞാൻ (njaan) — I
  • നീ (nee) — you (informal, for juniors and close friends)
  • നിങ്ങൾ (ningal) — you (polite, and plural)
  • അവൻ (avan) — he
  • അവൾ (aval) — she
  • അദ്ദേഹം (addeham) — he/she, respectful, for elders and officials
  • ഞങ്ങൾ (njangal) — we (not including the listener)
  • നമ്മൾ (nammal) — we (including the listener)
  • അവർ (avar) — they (also respectful singular)

Two things surprise learners. First, Malayalam has two words for "we" — one that includes the person you are talking to, one that does not. Second, politeness lives in the pronoun: when in doubt, always choose നിങ്ങൾ (ningal) for "you".

This, that, here and there

Pointing words come in matching pairs: ഇത് (ithu — this) and അത് (athu — that); ഇവിടെ (ivide — here) and അവിടെ (avide — there). The pattern is friendly: words beginning with "i" are near you, words beginning with "a" are further away — the same i/a pattern you saw in the question words.

Your first real questions

Now combine everything. "What is this?" — ഇത് എന്താണ്? (ithu enthaanu?). "Where is the bus stop?" — ബസ് സ്റ്റോപ്പ് എവിടെയാണ്? (bus stop evideyaanu?). "When does it start?" — എപ്പോൾ തുടങ്ങും? (eppol thudangum?). "How do I go to the station?" — സ്റ്റേഷനിലേക്ക് എങ്ങനെ പോകാം? (stationilekku engane pokaam?). Notice how the question word can sit in the middle of the sentence; Malayalam is flexible here, and people will understand you.

Build one new question a day and test it in the translator. Questions pair naturally with our everyday vocabulary — the words give you things to ask about.

Culture

Onam, Vishu and Kerala Festivals: Words and Wishes in Malayalam

Kerala celebrates often and celebrates together — Hindu, Christian and Muslim festivals fill the calendar, and everyone is invited to almost all of them. Knowing the words behind the celebrations turns a colourful spectacle into something you understand, and a correctly spoken festival wish earns you a place at the table.

Onam: the harvest homecoming

ഓണം (onam), in the month of Chingam (August–September), is Kerala's biggest festival, marking the mythical King Mahabali's yearly visit. Homes lay a flower carpet, the പൂക്കളം (pookkalam), wear new clothes called ഓണക്കോടി (onakkodi), and serve the great feast, the സദ്യ (sadhya) — dozens of vegetarian dishes on a banana leaf, eaten by hand. Wish everyone ഓണാശംസകൾ (onaashamsakal — Happy Onam), or more fully എല്ലാവർക്കും ഓണാശംസകൾ (ellaavarkkum onaashamsakal — Onam wishes to everyone).

Vishu: the new year of light

വിഷു (vishu), in mid-April, opens the Malayalam new year. The night before, families arrange the വിഷുക്കണി (vishukkani) — an auspicious display of rice, fruit, gold and a mirror — so it is the first thing seen at dawn. Elders give small gifts of money, കൈനേട്ടം (kaineettam), to children and dependents. The greeting is വിഷു ആശംസകൾ (vishu aashamsakal — Vishu greetings).

Temple festivals and Thrissur Pooram

Through the winter months, temples hold ഉത്സവം (uthsavam — festival) with caparisoned elephants, drum orchestras and fireworks; the most famous is Thrissur Pooram. A temple itself is a ക്ഷേത്രം (kshethram) or അമ്പലം (ambalam). Visitors are welcome at most celebrations outside the inner shrine; modest dress and removing footwear are the respectful basics.

Christmas, Eid and a shared table

Kerala's large Christian and Muslim communities add ക്രിസ്മസ് (christmas) — with star lanterns, carols and plum cake — and the two Eids, marked by ബിരിയാണി (biriyani) shared generously with neighbours of every faith. Wish ക്രിസ്മസ് ആശംസകൾ (christmas aashamsakal) in December and ഈദ് മുബാറക് (eid mubarak) at Eid. The shared word under all of it is ആശംസകൾ (aashamsakal — greetings, good wishes), which attaches to almost any celebration.

Sending wishes like a local

A short message in Malayalam script makes a big impression. Copy any wish from this page, or compose your own in the translator and send it as it is — no translation back needed. Add the person's name and ഒത്തിരി സ്നേഹത്തോടെ (othiri snehathode — with much love) for family and close friends.

Festivals are Kerala at its happiest. Learn the season's vocabulary with our days and months guide, and you will always know what is being celebrated — and what to say.

Speaking

How to Introduce Yourself in Malayalam

A good self-introduction is the highest-value minute of Malayalam you can learn. It answers the questions every new acquaintance in Kerala will ask — your name, your place, your work, your family — and it turns strangers into hosts. Build yours line by line below, then practise the complete example at the end.

Saying your name

The pattern is എന്റെ പേര് ... ആണ് (ente peru ... aanu — my name is ...). For example, എന്റെ പേര് അലക്സ് ആണ് (ente peru Alex aanu — my name is Alex). The word പേര് (peru) means name, and എന്റെ (ente) means my. Ask theirs in return: നിങ്ങളുടെ പേര് എന്താണ്? (ningalude peru enthaanu? — what is your name?).

Where you are from

Say ഞാൻ ... നിന്നാണ് (njaan ... ninnaanu — I am from ...): ഞാൻ ലണ്ടനിൽ നിന്നാണ് (njaan Londonil ninnaanu — I am from London). If you now live in Kerala, add ഇപ്പോൾ ഞാൻ കൊച്ചിയിലാണ് താമസം (ippol njaan Kochiyilaanu thaamasam — now I live in Kochi). Place names need no translation — Malayalam happily absorbs London, Dubai and Delhi as they are.

Age and family

Age uses the pattern എനിക്ക് ... വയസ്സായി (enikku ... vayassaayi — I am ... years old), with a number from our numbers guide. For family, borrow the frames from the family guide: എനിക്ക് ഒരു സഹോദരിയുണ്ട് (enikku oru sahodariyundu — I have one sister) is a complete, natural sentence.

Your work or studies

The frame is ഞാൻ ഒരു ... ആണ് (njaan oru ... aanu — I am a ...): ഞാൻ ഒരു ടീച്ചറാണ് (njaan oru teacher aanu — I am a teacher), ഞാൻ ഒരു വിദ്യാർത്ഥിയാണ് (njaan oru vidhyaarthiyaanu — I am a student). To name your workplace: ഞാൻ ... ൽ ജോലി ചെയ്യുന്നു (njaan ... il joli cheyyunnu — I work at ...). Job titles are usually borrowed from English, which makes this section the easiest of all.

Being honest about your Malayalam

Lower expectations gracefully with എനിക്ക് മലയാളം കുറച്ച് മാത്രമേ അറിയൂ (enikku malayalam kurachu maathrame ariyoo — I know only a little Malayalam), and invite patience: ദയവായി പതുക്കെ പറയൂ (dayavayi pathukke parayoo — please speak slowly). Keralites are famously encouraging language teachers; this sentence usually earns you a free lesson on the spot.

A complete example

Put it together: നമസ്കാരം! എന്റെ പേര് അലക്സ് ആണ്. ഞാൻ ലണ്ടനിൽ നിന്നാണ്. എനിക്ക് മുപ്പത് വയസ്സായി. ഞാൻ ഒരു ടീച്ചറാണ്. എനിക്ക് മലയാളം കുറച്ച് അറിയാം. നിങ്ങളെ കണ്ടതിൽ വളരെ സന്തോഷം! (Namaskaram! Ente peru Alex aanu. Njaan Londonil ninnaanu. Enikku muppathu vayassaayi. Njaan oru teacher aanu. Enikku malayalam kurachu ariyaam. Ningale kandathil valare santhosham!) — "Hello! My name is Alex. I am from London. I am thirty years old. I am a teacher. I know a little Malayalam. I am very happy to meet you!"

Now write your own version — swap in your name, place, age and job — and check it in the translator. Pair it with the greetings guide and your first Malayalam conversation is ready.
